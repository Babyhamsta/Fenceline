How municipal libraries digitized their card catalogs

In the late twentieth century, public libraries faced a quiet revolution. The wooden drawers of the card catalog, fixtures for generations of patrons, were gradually replaced by searchable electronic databases that promised faster lookups and remote access.

The transition was neither cheap nor simple. Librarians spent years transcribing millions of cards, reconciling inconsistent cataloging conventions, and training staff on unfamiliar terminals. Funding came in fits and starts, and many smaller branches lagged behind.

Yet the payoff was substantial. Once records were digital, a reader could search the entire collection from home, place holds, and discover related titles through linked subject headings. Interlibrary loan became dramatically more efficient.

Historians now study this period as an early example of institutional digitization, with lessons that echo in later efforts to scan books, preserve newspapers, and build the public archives that researchers rely on today.
